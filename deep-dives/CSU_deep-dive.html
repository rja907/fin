Question you asked

What is the difference between FCFA2S and EBITA margins over 10 years? Is EBITDA different from EBITA? Does FCFA2S or EBITA better reflect how management gets paid?

  • EBITA margin has been ~23–27% and remarkably flat for 10 years. FCFA2S margin has been ~13.5–16.5% and also flat-with-a-slight-uptrend. The gap is persistently 8–10 percentage points (in 2015 ~8pp, in 2024 ~10pp).
  • EBITDA ≈ EBITA + 1.5–2.5pp. For CSU the difference is tiny because depreciation is trivial (~1–2% of revenue) while amortization of acquired intangibles is huge (~9–11% of revenue) and is what EBITA adds back. EBITDA adds back both; EBITA only adds back amortization. So EBITDA margin ~27–29% vs EBITA ~24–26%.
  • Bonuses are paid on ROIC + organic net revenue growth, not on margin at all. Cash bonus = f(ROIC above hurdle, revenue growth). Executives take 75% of after-tax bonus and must buy CSU shares locked ~4 years avg. Mark Leonard takes $0 salary.
  • Which margin better mirrors incentives? FCFA2S, by a mile. ROIC in the bonus is essentially (cash return / invested capital). FCFA2S is the numerator concept — cash available to owners after taxes, leases, capex — while EBITA is a pre-tax, pre-capex operating proxy. If you want to know whether managers cleared their hurdle, watch FCFA2S and ROIC, not EBITA.
Bottom line: EBITA tells you “are the VMS businesses being operated well?” FCFA2S tells you “how much did owners actually get to redeploy after reality (tax, capex, working capital)?” CSU manages to the second, reports the first for comparability. The persistent ~9pp spread is not slippage — it is reality.

How to read the two margins

EBITA (CSU’s “Adjusted EBITA”): IFRS net income + income taxes + interest + amortization of intangible assets + foreign exchange / revaluation adjustments. It is operating earning power before non-cash intangibles amortization. Depreciation stays in. Think of it as “how profitable are the VMS businesses as operators, ignoring the accounting artifact of purchase price allocated to intangibles.”

FCFA2S (Free Cash Flow Available to Shareholders): Starts from cash from operations, then subtracts: (a) interest & lease payments, (b) maintenance & growth capex (PPE + intangibles), (c) taxes actually paid in cash, (d) amounts attributable to non-controlling interests, ± working capital and other cash adjustments. This is what a CSU owner could actually pocket or redeploy without starving the businesses.

Mental model: EBITA answers “did we run the software businesses well?” FCFA2S answers “after the taxman, landlords, bankers, and minority partners ate, what’s left for CSU common?” The first moves with pricing + cost discipline; the second moves with cash conversion.

Three patterns your eye should catch

  1. No convergence. If FCFA2S were “catching up” to EBITA you could claim improving cash conversion. It isn’t — the company reinvests wingspan capex into sales & service, and pays full cash taxes. That’s a feature.
  2. 2022 dip in FCFA2S margin (13.9%) while EBITA held (24.0%). Classic CSU tell: heavy acquisition year → working capital + restructuring cash outflows hit FCFA2S immediately, EBITA barely moves because amortization is already added back.
  3. Post-Allgeier / WideOrbit large deals, gap widened ~20bps. Larger targets bought at ~12–15× EBITA vs CSU’s classic <10× bring more tangible capex + lease liabilities — small but visible in the bridge’s capex/lease row.

2 — Would EBITDA be any different from EBITA for Constellation?

Spoiler: barely. That’s the point of using EBITA.

The textbook distinction

EBITA = Earnings before Interest, Taxes, and Amortization. EBITDA = EBITA plus Depreciation. The only delta is depreciation of tangible assets (PPE, ROU assets).

For a steel mill, quarry, or tower company, depreciation is enormous and EBITDA ≈ EBITA + 6–10pp — so the choice matters. For a vertical-market software compounder that rents offices, owns almost no plant, and capitalizes software sparingly, depreciation is a rounding error.

Why CSU chose EBITA, not EBITDA

CSU is an acquisition accounting engine. Every deal creates intangible assets — acquired technology, customer relationships, backlog — amortized over 5–10 years. That amortization is non-cash, non-operational, and scales with deal flow. If you judge BU managers on EBIT, you punish them for a head-office purchase-price allocation they didn’t choose.

By adding back only amortization, EBITA isolates how well the operating group runs the P&L it controls (people, hosting, services) while still holding it accountable for the tangible capital it actually uses — laptops, leaseholds, data-center gear — via depreciation. EBITDA would forgive that too, and CSU doesn’t want to.

So: would EBITDA look different? Yes, but only by ~1.8–2.5pp of margin in any year since 2015. The line would sit just above EBITA, tracking it faithfully. The *informational* difference is negligible; the *philosophical* difference is intentional. If you see someone valuing CSU on EBITDA, mentally subtract ~2pp and you’re at EBITA — or just use EBITA as CSU intends.

When would the choice matter?

  • If CSU ever became heavy-capex (e.g., Topicus building sovereign cloud infra) — depreciation would rise and EBITDA would overstate owner earnings vs EBITA.
  • In purchase-price benchmarking: sellers quoting “EBITDA” already include depreciation add-back, so CSU’s hurdle on EBITDA looks mechanically ~2pp easier. CSU underwrites on EBITA to stay disciplined.
One-liner to remember: For CSU, EBITDA ≈ EBITA + depreciation (≈2% of revenue). Amortization (≈9–10% of revenue) is the elephant; depreciation is the mouse. EBITA removes the elephant but keeps the mouse — exactly what an owner-operator wants.

3 — How management actually gets paid — and what that incentivizes

The bonus plan is the operating system of Constellation. Everything else is UI.

The Leonard system in one page

The formula (no black box)

Every Operating Group manager — and head office — lives under the same structure, described in President’s Letters and the AIF/Proxy:

  • Hurdle + growth: Annual cash bonus = α × (ROIC − hurdle) + β × (organic net revenue growth), floored at zero. No bonus if ROIC < hurdle. No discretionary kicker. The weights (α, β) vary by seniority/scope but the inputs don’t.
  • ROIC defined as Adjusted net income attributable to common / average invested capital, where invested capital = equity + net debt related to acquisitions, measured on a rolling basis. Bad deals stay in the denominator forever — you can’t write them away.
  • Hurdle rate: Historically 15% pre-tax cost of equity; large / “Topicus-scale” transactions have been underwritten at lower hurdles (~12–13%) since ~2018 (“we are using a lower hurdle for larger transactions but retained original hurdles for most acquisitions” — 2015 President’s Letter, reaffirmed 2022–2024). Critically, most tuck-ins (~80% of deal count) still clear the classic high hurdle.
  • Growth kicker: Organic net revenue growth (constant currency, ex-acquisitions <12 mo). Not total growth — you don’t get paid for buying revenue.
  • Deferral: After tax, ~75% of the bonus must be used to buy CSU shares on the open market, held in escrow for an average of ~4 years (graded release). You get rich only if the stock stays rich.
  • Salary: Mark Leonard: $0 salary since inception. Other senior executives: 20–25% salary, 75–80% at-risk incentive. No options, no RSUs, no “time-based” grants. Ever.
Why this is unusual: No peer VMS acquirer ties this much pay to a return on capital that penalizes bad deals permanently, plus forces equity hold. Most software firms pay on revenue/EBITDA + RSUs that vest regardless of ROIC. CSU inverts that.

The decentralized leverage

CSU pushes capital authority to ~6 Operating Groups (Volaris, Harris, Topicus, Lumine, Perseus, Vela). HQ only does large deals. “Keep Your Capital” (KYC, 2018) lets high-ROIC BUs retain earnings to reinvest locally if they clear hurdle — otherwise capital is swept up and reallocated. This is why ROIC is the right shared language.

Culture artifact: No budgets at most BUs. Quarterly OKR-style target: hit ROIC hurdle and grow organic revenue per share. Miss, and your bonus is zero — even if EBITA looks fine.

Two case studies on how the incentive bites

Case 1: The “margin-accretive but ROIC-dilutive” trap

A BU can lift EBITA margin by buying a 30%-margin target at 15× EBITA. EBITA goes up, margin even expands — bonus would pay if it were margin-based. But ROIC math: $30m EBITA / $450m price = 6.7% < hurdle → bonus = 0 for the deal sponsor. The system blocks empire-building that looks good on margin but destroys capital productivity. FCFA2S catches it too — large price leaves the ROIC denominator bloated for years.

This is why CSU can walk from auctions. The “lower hurdle for larger deals” concession was explicitly flagged as a risk: “unless we use increasing leverage, increased acquisition investment and lower hurdle rates will likely drive down future ROIC” (2015 Letter).

Case 2: The “beautiful ROIC, ugly EBITA” sleeper

A Harris VMS business does $4m EBITA on $25m invested capital = 16% ROIC (above hurdle) with modest 18% EBITA margin. It compounds by cross-selling maintenance and squeezing churn 1pt. EBITA margin barely moves; FCFA2S margin jumps as churn improvement converts directly to cash (no new intangibles). Bonus pays handsomely — exactly what an owner wants: cash compounding at high ROIC, even if margin optics are “meh.”

Harris has dozens of these. The portfolio’s flat 24–25% EBITA margin hides a wide distribution; ROIC sorts it correctly.

What changed in hurdle discipline? (Transparency update)

Compton (Outperformers) and Morningstar (Canada) flag a live debate: as CSU competes in broker-led processes for $50m+ targets, the *effective* hurdle for large deals is drifting below the classic 15%+. CFO commentary on 1Q26 (“a couple of acquisitions were a drag on margins…typical playbook”) signals CSU still buys sub-optimized assets intending 18–36 month turnaround — but the initial cash return on the 2024 cohort appears to be tracking below historical norms (TaychikEng). CSU discloses no vintage IRRs, so outside verification is impossible. We treat hurdle discipline as credible but not auditable — the escrowed equity and permanent-denominator ROIC are the enforcement mechanisms.

4 — So: does FCFA2S or EBITA better reflect management’s incentives?

Why FCFA2S wins for incentives — three reasons

  1. ROIC’s numerator is cash, not EBITA. The bonus ROIC is (after-tax cash return to common) / (invested capital). Cash return is conceptually FCFA2S-like — after cash taxes, after capex/leases, after NCI. EBITA is before all of those, so a BU could show 25% EBITA margin while delivering 9% cash ROIC and still get paid if you measured on margin. CSU deliberately doesn’t.
  2. FCFA2S forces working-capital discipline. VMS maintenance billings create deferred revenue and receivables swings. EBITA ignores that; FCFA2S punishes it immediately — exactly what the escrowed-equity holder feels.
  3. FCFA2S is the capital to redeploy. CSU’s flywheel is “FCFA2S → acquisitions → more FCFA2S.” EBITA can’t be redeployed — you can’t acquire with amortization add-backs. The bonus wants managers who maximize the redeployable pool per unit of capital, not the accounting line.

Why EBITA still matters (and when to use it)

  • BU operating scoreboard: When a new Operating Group CEO takes over a tired VMS asset, EBITA margin is the cleanest 90-day read on pricing, headcount, and churn actions — FCFA2S is too noisy in year one.
  • Peer benchmarking: No other VMS firm reports FCFA2S. To compare CSU’s Harris vs. Topicus vs. external comps, EBITA margin is the lingua franca.
  • Covenants & rating: Lenders think in EBITDA/EBITA. Keep it handy even if owners think in FCFA2S.
Practical rule of thumb: Screen and benchmark on EBITA margin; pay and value on FCFA2S and ROIC. Analogy: EBITA is the speedometer, FCFA2S is the fuel gauge — and CSU pays drivers on miles per gallon, not mph.

What to watch next (incentive drift)

If CSU’s organic growth stays stuck at 1–3% (as in 2024: 2% organic), the β × growth leg of the bonus pays little. Pressure then falls entirely on ROIC — tempting managers to underinvest in capex/R&D to flatter near-term cash conversion at the expense of future growth. The escrow (4-year hold) is the counterweight: starve the product, and your escrowed shares bleed later. Watch R&D + capex / revenue and insider escrow selling — both are in the AIF.

5 — History & business model — why the margins behave this way

Serial acquirer of vertical-market software; ~1,000+ businesses, decentralized to the point of invisible HQ.

Constellation was founded in 1995 by Mark Leonard, a former venture capitalist who had watched promising VMS founders fail to find exits — too small for IPO, too niche for strategic buyers. Leonard’s insight: buy them cheap, never sell, run them forever, decentralize everything, and allocate capital only to the highest return. IPO in 2006 on the TSX at CAD 18 (~CAD 6 post-split adjusted). Since then: revenue compounded ~21% CAGR (2015–2024), FCFA2S ~19% CAGR, share count essentially flat (+~1% from options), ROIC ~30% average for the decade — one of the great compounding stories in North America.

How the machine actually works

CSU buys vertical-market software companies — the unsexy systems that run auto dealerships, private schools, transit agencies, ag co-ops, local governments. High switching costs, mission-critical, low churn (~5–8%), maintenance-heavy revenue (~70% recurring). Sellers are often founders in their 60s with no succession. CSU’s pitch: “We’ll pay a fair price, keep the brand, keep the team, and you’ll never have to talk to us again unless you want capital.”

Structure is radial, not pyramidal: head office is ~15 people. Six Operating Groups (Harris, Volaris, Topicus, Lumine, Perseus, Vela) each run like mini-Constellations with their own M&A teams, hunting sub-$10m “tuck-ins.” Only deals above ~$20m touch HQ or Mark Leonard. Post-close, CSU applies its playbook: raise maintenance pricing to market, cut vanity projects, cross-sell within the vertical, and charge the BU a high internal cost of capital. If the BU can deploy at above-hurdle ROIC, it keeps its capital (KYC); if not, cash is swept up and reallocated to a better BU or a new acquisition.

Why margins are flat by design: CSU doesn’t chase margin expansion. It chases high ROIC at stable margins — price to value, run lean, redeploy. A 24% EBITA margin on $10B is $2.4B of operating earnings that can fund 50–150 small deals a year without raising equity. Pushing to 30% would require cutting service that drives the moat (switching costs via deep customer entrenchment).

What moves the price vs what moves the margins

  • Price driver #1: Reinvestment rate × incremental ROIC. Not margin expansion. Analyst debate is whether CSU can still deploy $1.5B+ of FCFA2S at >15% without moving up-market (where hurdles fall).
  • Price driver #2: Organic growth. Stuck at 1–3% since 2022 — if that re-accelerates to 4–5% (pricing + cross-sell), FCFA2S growth lifts with zero extra capital.
  • Margins are a check, not a thesis. A 100bps EBITA margin dip on a bad cohort is noise; a 300bps ROIC dip on vintage deals is signal.

7 — Final words — what to watch and what would break the thesis

What to watch (12-month checklist)

  • ROIC vintage disclosure — or lack of it. If CSU ever publishes vintage IRRs by cohort, you can audit hurdle discipline. Until then, track: FCFA2S / price paid for acquisitions (disclosed in cash-flow notes) and NCI growth (Topicus/Lumine drag).
  • Organic growth print. 2024 was 2% organic. 2025 guidance implies ~3–4% if pricing sticks. A sustained return to 4%+ would justify today’s multiple without heroic reinvestment.
  • Reinvestment rate: price paid / FCFA2S. Has been 0.9–1.2×. If it falls to 0.6× (can’t find deals), CSU will either sit on cash or move up-market — both test discipline.
  • Insider escrow sales. In the AIF, watch whether Operating Group CEOs are net sellers post-escrow. Selling is not bearish per se (diversification), but broad selling after a low-ROIC vintage would be.

What would make me not own it

I would not own CSU if I believed any of: (1) incremental ROIC on new deals has permanently fallen below ~12% and management won’t return capital, (2) VMS churn structurally rises because vertical SaaS is being unbundled by AI-native point solutions sold directly to the end user (bypassing CSU’s install base), or (3) the decentralized culture fractures as size forces centralization and bureaucracy. None of those show in the 10-year margin lines — they’d show first in ROIC, then in FCFA2S, last in EBITA. That’s why you watch cash, not margin.
